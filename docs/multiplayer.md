# Multiplayer

How online games work for the players, and how to run and host the relay they connect through. The game itself is described in [game.md](game.md).

## Skipping a round

A round that has gone nowhere can be skipped by vote. Only the **host** may call it (*Vote to skip round* in the menu, or **N**); the call is the host's own yes. Everybody at the table, the host included, then answers with **Y** / **N**, or with the buttons that come up on a touch screen. More than half of the people (CPUs have no vote) have to say yes within 20 seconds; if too many say no, or time runs out, play goes on. A skipped round ends at once with the title *Round skipped*: **nobody scores in it** – every ship goes back to the points it had when the round began, bets on the round are returned, and oracle points and honours from the skipped round are discarded – and the match goes on with the next round (or the final screen after the last one). A skipped round still counts towards the number of rounds, and the host reports it to the relay's statistics like any round, under the title `skipped` (with the scores it ended on, and only the turns that were really flown: a volley still in the air is dropped). A replay of such a game ends that round after its last recorded turn, because the relay hands the client the list of skipped rounds along with the turns.

Offline there is no vote: the player skips on their own (*Skip round* in the pause menu, or **N** twice).

## Playing online

In the client, open **Multiplayer**, connect to the relay address (default `ws://localhost:8080`), then create or join a room.

- **Rooms:** choose Classic or Event Horizon, free-for-all or two-team play (everybody picks their own team in the room, the host can deal everybody out again with *Reset teams*) and the rules of the game (rounds, planets, reflective edges, shot power, flight time, betting on the round, power-ups with their kinds and amount, …). A **Mayhem** room is a third kind: it is played in Classic or Event Horizon, its rules are only the rounds, the chaos strength, the flight time and (in Classic) simultaneous shots, with trick-shot bonuses always on, and every round plays by twists of its own (see [game.md](game.md#mayhem)). Both modes support up to six players. Rooms can be protected with a password. The host can fill seats with **CPUs** (Kepler, Newton, Einstein or Hawking, the offline levels) while the room waits: they are seated and changed in the room, always ready, and run on the host's machine, so they need no connection of their own. A room with CPUs still has room for a person: when it is full, the newest CPU gives its seat to whoever joins. A game needs at least two people, so CPUs alone are played in the singleplayer lobby instead. Rooms are numbered, and each has a symbol and a colour from a fixed set of choices. A room is either **public** (listed in the lobby) or **private** (not listed; it is joined with its code, which the room shows to everybody inside). Player names are the only free text.
- **Invite links:** the room carries a *Copy invite link* button next to its code. The link opens the client and puts the guest into the room by itself — it carries the room's code, so nothing has to be typed or clicked. The host's link for a locked room carries its password in the URL fragment; guests in locked rooms see the code and can join by hand from the join row. A link from a build without a relay of its own carries the relay the room runs on. An invite that names a different relay asks for a click before connecting, and does not change the saved relay unless the player confirms by connecting.
- **Symbols:** every online player picks a symbol – a rocket, satellite, alien, robot, astronaut, crosshair, bolt or gem. It is separate from the room's symbol, and shown in the lobby, above the ship in the game and on the player's card. The colour stays the one of the player's seat, as in the rest of the game.
- **Host:** the host can change the rules, and the room's visibility, symbol and colour, and seat, level, team or remove CPUs, while the room is waiting, and starts the game. Every change to the seats makes the people confirm again. The host runs the authoritative simulation and relays state patches to the other players – only what changed since the last tick, so a match needs a few KB/s instead of MB/s. Guests carry a shot's flight on between patches, so it moves smoothly.
- **Pausing:** the host can pause a running game from the menu (*Pause game*). Everything stops for everybody, the shot clock and the CPUs included, and inputs are ignored; a *Game paused* banner shows on every screen. *Resume game* starts a 3-2-1 countdown before play goes on. The pause is part of the game state the host sends, so people who join mid-pause see it too, and the relay needs no knowledge of it.
- **Wire format:** lobby and control messages use MessagePack in binary WebSocket frames. Frequent game states use a versioned binary envelope (`SLNG`, version, kind, sequence) followed by an opaque MessagePack `{patch, events}` payload, allowing the relay to check the sequence and forward the payload without decoding/re-encoding it. Older clients and servers are not wire-compatible and must be upgraded together.
- **Event Horizon online:** everybody aims at the same time against one shared clock.
- **Oracle:** whoever is shot down does not just wait. Turn by turn the oracle asks "Will Lena hit?", with simultaneous shots (Event Horizon, or Classic with *Simultaneous shots*) "Who gets hit?". One card at the bottom edge asks the question and takes the tip until the shots are away; right tips pay 100 / 150 (a named ship) / 75 (nobody), and every right tip in a row raises the multiplier up to ×3. The oracle's own standing is a side standing – it never counts towards winning – and the oracle is always on in online games. A wrong tip costs nothing but the streak; the one exception is a bet on the round, below, which the host has to allow. The host judges the tips, so a relay older than this feature rejects them. On top of that:
  - **Face down:** the other shot-down players' tips lie face down on the card ("● Dora ?") – everybody sees *that* someone tipped, nobody sees *what* – and turn over one after the other when the shots are away, so there is nothing to copy. The wire carries them face down as well – only who tipped, plus the hit/miss tally behind the mood below. A right tip against the crowd (with at least three tipsters, fewer than half named the same) pays 50 on top.
  - **The ghosts' mood:** the living see how the ghosts lean on the shot in the air – a ring and a note over the shooter's ship ("The ghosts don't believe in you (3 of 4)"), not who tipped what. When the majority of the tipsters turns out wrong the field announces *Oracle fooled!*; when three or more all agreed and were right, *The oracle knew!*
  - **Who wins the round?:** as soon as somebody is out and two or more ships are still in, a second question opens as a quieter row of the same card and stays open for the rest of the round. Tip with Shift + 1–5 or by tapping, as long as the next shots have not flown (changing your mind is fine); a tip pays 100 for every rival the winner had to beat when the tip was made (two ships left pay 100, five pay 400), a draw tipped as "nobody" pays 200. With teams, the whole winning team wins. The others only see how many have tipped; after the round the answers are turned over on the card.
  - **Betting on the round:** with the room rule *Betting on the round* switched on (the host sets it while the room is waiting; it is off by default, since it moves points of the match), a shot-down player who has points can put some of them on their tip on the round question – once per round, after tipping; once points ride on it, the tip stays. The stakes are a share of the score: half of the most allowed, or the most, which is half of the score (at least 20). They leave the score at once and **do count towards the match**. All the stakes of a round make one pot: whoever tipped right shares it in proportion to their stakes, whoever tipped wrong loses theirs. If nobody was right, everybody was, or only one bet, everybody just gets their stake back. Payouts come in steps of ten, and the odd steps go to the larger shares, so the whole pot is paid out. In a team match the winners are the whole team, whichever of its ships was tipped, and the tip's points count teams in, not ships. The odds are whatever the other bettors make them – the pot is shown beside the stake, and what everybody tipped stays face down until the round is over. At the end of the round the card lists the bets, and what they came to goes into the scores and the history chart.
  - **Honours:** the round's and the match's best get a title – *Prophet* (most points), *Seer* (longest run of right tips), *Lone wolf* (right against the crowd), *Optimist* / *Pessimist* (almost always "hit" / "miss"), *Unlucky* (longest run of wrong tips) – but only when the lead is clear. At the end of a round the card turns into the round's summary – who won the round question, everybody's tip turned over, the standings and the honours, one at a time – and the honours are listed on the final screen.
- **Spectators:** anybody can watch a room from the stands instead of taking a seat – up to 20 per room. *Watch* is on every room in the list (and next to the room code for private rooms). A room that is still waiting can be watched with its password like it can be joined; **a game that is already running can be joined mid-round when its room is public and has no password** – those rooms stay in the list, marked *Playing*, with the number of people watching. Spectators see exactly what the players see, take no seat and are not counted among the people a game needs, so they can come and go without ending anything; the host's room closing sends them home. They cannot aim, fire or change anything; the relay only passes on their bets (and rate-limits them). Late arrivals have nothing to apply the host's state patches to, so the relay asks the host for one complete frame (`keyframe`) – a short burst of bandwidth, once per newcomer; a connection may walk into the stands three times in 30 seconds. With *Hidden aim* on, spectators see no aims either. Everybody else sees only a small count of how many are watching.
  - **Betting from the stands:** spectators play the oracle's own tipping game, with the same card, keys and taps as the shot-down players (see above): hit or miss on each shot, who gets hit in a salvo, who wins the round (Shift + 1–5), and a stake on that tip (B / Shift + B). Two differences: they have no ship to lose, so every question is open from the start of the round as soon as somebody is watching, and they bet with a bankroll of their own – 500 points per match, kept by the host – instead of match points, so the stakes need no room rule and nothing a spectator does touches a player's score. The stands have an oracle of their own, so their pot is separate from the ghosts'. **The odds come from the bets:** all stakes of the round make one pot and whoever tipped right shares it in proportion to their stakes (everybody gets their stake back when nobody, or everybody, was right), and each side on the round row shows the pot divided by what is staked on it ("Anna ×2.4"), live – in the stands' book what is staked on each side is public, who tipped what stays face down. Teammates share one side's odds. Somebody almost broke starts the next round with at least 100 points. The final screen lists the stands' standings as *Best seers in the stands*. The points are for bragging only, and live on the host: a spectator who leaves and comes back gets a new place and a fresh 500.
- **Chat:** everybody in a room – players and spectators – can talk, in the waiting room and during the game, in a panel docked beside the waiting room’s player slots and an expandable panel during online matches. The in-game button shows an unread count and remembers whether it was expanded. Chat is hidden on the launcher, room browser, local games, Daily, settings and tutorials, even if a session still holds a room. Press Escape to collapse in-game chat and give the keyboard back to the game. The relay does the checking: a message is stripped of control and invisible characters, cut to 200 characters, and run through the same profanity filter as names, which stars out the objectionable words (`s***`; like names, the dictionary is English); nobody can send more than five messages in ten seconds. The host can switch on the room rule *Chat: players only*: spectators then still read everything but cannot write. A refused message is answered in the chat itself. Chat lives only in the room: there is no history, and a spectator who joins sees what is said from then on. **The CPUs talk back.** The host's client flies the CPUs, so it also speaks for them: after a near miss (a shot that passes an enemy hull within 45 px), a kill, a death or an own goal, a CPU may say a line in its own voice (Kepler, Newton, Einstein and Hawking each have five lines per occasion, some of them quotes from the namesake), sent as `cpu_chat` with the seat's `playerId` and shown to the room as that seat's chat line. The line is in the host's language. The relay accepts it only from the host, during a running game, for a CPU seat, cleans and censors it like any chat, and allows four a ten seconds, apart from the host's own chat budget. The client keeps the CPUs from chattering: a pause after anyone spoke, a longer one for the same CPU, and a chance of silence for the less dramatic occasions.
- **Lobby:** it remembers your server, name, and room settings and room look (separate from the settings of offline games) and reconnects on its own. Leaving a game puts you back into the lobby. A build whose `VITE_MULTIPLAYER_SERVER` is a `wss://` address (the hosted one) has no connection screen: it connects as the lobby opens, and only shows a way to try again when that fails. Other builds keep the connection card, with its server address and connect and disconnect buttons.

## Dropped connections

A connection that drops in the middle of a game (a Wi-Fi hiccup, a corporate proxy recycling its sockets) does not end the game.

- **The seat is held.** The relay keeps a dropped player's seat in a *running* game for 30 seconds, the host's included, and tells the room ("Anna lost the connection"; the seat is marked `connected: false` in `room_update`). The game carries on in the host's client; only if the seat is not taken back in time does the old behaviour apply (a guest's seat ends the game, the host's closes the room). A drop in a waiting room still frees the seat at once, and so does leaving on purpose (`leave_room`: the Disconnect button, and closing the tab, which sends it on `pagehide`). The client's resume window and the relay's hold are both 30 s; the relay's sweep runs every 2 s, so it lets go up to 2 s later.
- **Taking it back.** A seated player's `room_update` carries a `you.token`, a random secret for the seat. After a drop the client keeps its room and match, connects again with growing pauses (0.25 s up to 3 s) for up to 30 seconds and sends `resume` with the token. The relay moves the seat to the new connection, tells the room ("Anna is back") and, for a guest, asks the host for a complete frame (`keyframe`); a returning host starts its patches over from a full snapshot. If the seat was given up meanwhile the relay answers `resume_failed` and the client goes back to the lobby.
- **Quiet connections.** The relay pings every 10 s and drops a peer after three pings without any sign of life (any message counts, not just the pong). A seated guest that hears nothing from a connected host for 6 s drops its own socket and reconnects the same way (spectators have no seat to take back and are not asked to). A host in a hidden tab draws no frames, so a 1 s heartbeat timer keeps sending its state meanwhile.
- **Logs.** The relay logs why a connection ended (`peer closed the connection`, `connection read failed`, `no sign of life after the pings`, `outbound backlog limit exceeded`), and when a seat is held, resumed or expires; with `RUST_LOG=info` this tells a dropped proxy (many at once) from one player's network.

## Running the relay

Start the relay with `npm run dev:server` (needs the Rust toolchain; `PORT` overrides `8080`).

Set `ALLOWED_ORIGINS` to the frontend's exact origin before startup. For Vite, run `ALLOWED_ORIGINS=http://localhost:5173 npm run dev:server`; `npm run dev:server` defaults to localhost and 127.0.0.1 origins on ports 5173, 5174 and 4173 for development. Other deployments must configure an allowlist; the relay refuses to start without one unless `ALLOW_ALL_ORIGINS=1`.
The Rust relay logs through `tracing`. Set `RUST_LOG=debug` to show each message and room event. The default level (`info`) shows lifecycle events. The relay accepts MessagePack binary frames; text JSON frames are rejected, so deploy the matching client and server versions together.

**Hosting.** For remote players, host both the web client and the relay at reachable addresses. Use a `wss://` address behind TLS when the client is served over HTTPS. Set `VITE_MULTIPLAYER_SERVER` to that address when building the client: it is embedded in the build, and a `wss://` value turns on the hosted lobby described above.

**Relay environment.** `PORT` selects the listening port (default `8080`). `ALLOWED_ORIGINS` is a comma-separated exact-origin allowlist; `ALLOW_ALL_ORIGINS=1` disables origin checks. Set both `TLS_CERT` and `TLS_KEY` to PEM file paths to serve WSS directly; if TLS terminates at a reverse proxy, leave both unset and forward to the relay over a trusted network. `MAX_CONNECTIONS` (8192), `MAX_CONNECTIONS_PER_IP` (64), `CONNECTIONS_PER_IP_PER_SECOND` (20), and `MAX_MESSAGES_PER_SECOND` (300) tune admission and per-connection rate limits. `MAX_BYTES_PER_SECOND` (2 MiB) caps what one connection may send, with room for one full 1 MiB frame at any time; a real host streams well under 1 MB/s. A connection over either rate limit is closed with code 1008. Messages other than the host's state are refused above 256 KiB. `MAX_OUTBOUND_BYTES` (512 MiB) bounds what all outbound queues hold together, on top of each connection's 4 MiB; a connection whose frame would overflow either budget is dropped as a slow client. The per-address limits count a **client**: an IPv4 address, or an IPv6 address by its /64 (an IPv4 address the dual-stack listener reports as IPv4-mapped counts as the IPv4 address), so a host cannot dodge them by cycling through the addresses of its own network; the daily board and the statistics tell players apart the same way. **Browser id:** so that the people behind one shared address (a VPN, a carrier NAT) do not share the abuse limits, the game keeps a random 128-bit id in its own storage (`slingshot.browser.v1`) and adds it to every connection (`?b=`). The limits on wrong passwords, password derivations, database requests, statistics rows and local games count per browser, under a ceiling of 10 times as much per client; a connection without an id counts as one browser of its client, so leaving the id out or making ids up gains nothing past the ceiling. The relay keeps the id in memory for as long as a limit's window and never writes it to the database, logs it or uses it for anything else (the board and the statistics stay keyed by the client). Room passwords: a browser may type 10 wrong ones in 15 minutes, over all its connections and rooms, before it is turned away until they age out; a room takes 5 password checks per 5 seconds from everybody together; a browser runs at most one password derivation at a time, and a client two. The room list goes out to the lobby at most every 250 ms (the latest list always does), and a connection may ask for it 5 times in 10 seconds. Requests that reach the database (the daily board, the statistics page, the menu's facts, replays) are counted per browser, `DB_REQUESTS_PER_MINUTE` (120) a minute; the game opens a connection per request, so a per-connection count would not hold. The board takes runs only for the days it keeps and for tomorrow (already today east of UTC); any other day has an empty board and costs no read. A day's top list is read at most once a second, and the latest 16 replays are kept in memory. `STATS_ROWS_PER_DAY` (300 000) is how many rows of statistics one browser may have written per day, hosting and local games together: a real host writes about ten a turn (`npx tsx benchmarks/report-volume.ts` measures it), so a day of hosting stays near a third of it, and reports past it are dropped with one warning. Pruning the board and the statistics runs in a task of its own, never in the accept loop. These limits use the TCP peer address; configure proxy/network limits accordingly. `DATABASE` names the relay's one SQLite file (default `slingshot.db`, relative to the working directory; `LEADERBOARD_DB`, its old name, still works) and `LEADERBOARD_KEEP_DAYS` how many days of the daily board in it are kept (default `7`); a file that cannot be opened stops the relay at startup, so a bad path fails the deployment instead of running on without one. Match statistics are **off unless asked for**: start the relay with `--stats` (`npm run dev:server:stats`), or set `STATS=1` where a service has no arguments to give. They go into the same file as the board, and `STATS_KEEP_DAYS` is how many days of them are kept (default `7`). `BACKUP_DIR` switches on a daily snapshot of the file (off when unset): once a UTC day, on the first sweep tick, the relay writes a consistent copy with `VACUUM INTO` to `slingshot-YYYY-MM-DD.db` in that directory (created if missing; one that cannot be made stops the relay like a bad `DATABASE`) and deletes all but the newest `BACKUP_KEEP` (default `14`). It runs in the pruning task, never in the accept loop. `RUST_LOG` controls log filtering. `npm run bench:relay -- ws://localhost:8080` sends the localhost Vite origin by default; override it with `BENCH_ORIGIN` when benchmarking another allowlisted origin. Its synthetic large-state case streams about 8 MB/s, so start the relay with `MAX_BYTES_PER_SECOND=67108864` to measure it; under the default the relay closes that host after a few frames.

**Names in the lobby.** A player name is held to the same profanity check as board and room names, when joining or creating (`That name is not allowed`) and when renaming. A player renames from the field above their symbol picker in a waiting room (`set_name`); it is refused while a game runs.

## Daily leaderboard

After a daily run, the result card offers **Post to leaderboard** — but only where the build was given a relay (`VITE_MULTIPLAYER_SERVER`). The button opens the day's board with your run already in hand: type a name (remembered for next time, the same one the lobby uses), press *Post score*, and your best run for that day stands on the board with your own row marked. The Daily Challenge screen has a **Leaderboard** row of its own, which opens the same board read-only — no name, nothing to press but Back — so the day's standing can be looked at before or without flying it.

- **One row per player and day**, kept at the player's best. A later, worse run leaves the row alone; a better one takes the name it was posted under along with it. The board shows the best 50 runs of the day; your own place is shown even when it is further down.
- **The relay identifies a player by their address**, hashed together with the day, so a *household or any shared connection has one row for the day* and no address is ever written to the board file. Nothing else is kept about a poster.
- **A name the relay will not carry is refused, not edited**, and the player is told so on the spot — the words they typed stay in the field with the cursor in it, so the fix is retyping one thing and pressing Enter again. The check is `rustrict` at its *inappropriate* threshold, which folds in the usual evasions (`f4ck`, `sh1t`, `a55hole`) as well as the plain words, and reads three or more x's as the adult-content marker. Two limits come with it and neither is a reason to drop it: the dictionary is English with a German list added (`protocol.rs`: profanity from the German list of LDNOOBW, CC BY 4.0, cut down to words that do not turn up inside ordinary ones, and a few stems like `brau` and `kanal` cleared so `brauchen` and `Kanal` pass), so German is covered only in part (`Arsch` and `Hurensohn` are caught; the bare surname `Fick` is not), and it reads no context, so a handful of real surnames come out unacceptable (`Hancock`, `Dick`). Names are also trimmed of control characters and cut to 24 units before any of this.
- **The scores are taken at face value.** A run is reported by the player's own browser and the relay has no way to fly it again, so it only bounds what it accepts: a real calendar day, an acceptable name, a whole score from `0` to `10,000,000` (`maxScore` in `protocol-contract.json`, alongside `boardSize`, `nameLength` and `keepDays`). It is a board for a friendly daily, not a ranking to win.
- **The boards are pruned**, not accumulated: a relay sweep drops the days past `LEADERBOARD_KEEP_DAYS` at most every six hours, so the file stays the size of a week's worth of runs.

The board shares one SQLite-compatible file beside the relay with the match statistics (each has its tables and its own connection), read and written through [`turso`](https://crates.io/crates/turso), the Rust engine — so the build needs no C toolchain for it, only for the TLS stack it already had. It rides on the relay's WebSocket protocol as two extra control messages, `daily_submit` and `daily_top`, answered with `daily_board`. Nothing about them is stored per connection: the board is read and written on demand, and a client opens a short-lived connection for one request of its own.

## Match statistics

For a local UI preview, run `npm run dev:server:stats-demo` and start Vite with `VITE_MULTIPLAYER_SERVER=ws://localhost:8080 npm run dev`, then open `/stats`. `--stats-demo` implies `--stats` and uses a separate `slingshot-stats-demo.db` by default (override with `STATS_DEMO_DATABASE`); it rebuilds the synthetic history each time and ignores `DATABASE` and `LEADERBOARD_DB`.

When started with `--stats`, the relay keeps a year of numbers about online games, to see how they are played: how long rounds run, which rules and game modes get picked, how aim and power-ups pay off, how often CPUs win. The relay itself never sees a game — the host's client runs it and the state frames are opaque to the relay — so the host reports, and it does so turn by turn:

- **After every turn** (a turn is one volley: a single shot in classic's turn order, everybody's shots when they fly together) the host's client sends `turn_stats`: the turn number, every shot fired in it (see `stat_shots` below) and, for each seat, the score and whether the ship is still alive.
- **When a round ends** it sends `round_stats`: how the round ended, its length, the planet count, for each seat the score, shots, kills, swing-bys, grazes, own goals, pick-ups and shield saves and whether it survived, and the round's records (longest shot, fastest kill, best hit, narrowest miss, longest hit), and — in a room with tipping (the shot-down players' and the stands' oracle) — how each tip and bet judged in the round came out (`tips`: no names, only the kind, right or wrong, the stake and what it paid).

The relay adds what it knows itself: the game and room mode, the room's rules, how many seats are people and how many CPUs (and which level), and how many are watching.

- **No names.** Not a player's, not a room's; the report carries seat numbers and the relay writes none of the names it knows. A room appears only as `room_id`, 128 random bits made with the room and never used again (not the short code players type to join, which is free once the room is gone), so it names one room, ever, and nobody in it. A person is told apart by a keyed hash (HMAC-SHA256) of their address *and the name they play under*, so the *same person is the same player on every day of a week and after every restart*, and a new player every Sunday (UTC), when the salt is replaced, two people behind one address are two players, and one person under two names is two. The key is a random salt made on the first start of a week and kept in the database (`stat_meta`, with the week's first day) – **it sits next to the rows**, so anyone who has the file can try addresses and names against it; the hash keeps names and addresses out of the rows, not out of reach of the file's owner. It is not the board's key (the board hashes address and day with a key held in memory), so a row here can never be matched to a name on the board. A CPU seat has no hash, only its level. Rounds of one game share a random `match_id`, so several games in one room can be told apart.
- **Reported by the host, in order.** The relay takes a report only from the host of a running game and only with one entry per seat. A turn is written down once and only after the turns before it (turns count from 1 in every round), and once a round is reported its turns are over; numbers out of range, a shot of some other turn or a title, resolution or power-up it does not know are refused. Like the daily board, the numbers are taken at face value: they come from the host's own browser.
- **Pruned after `STATS_KEEP_DAYS`** (default 365) by the same six-hourly sweep as the board. A year of every shot and turn is a lot of rows on a busy relay – `npm run bench:stats` shows what a row costs – so lower it if the file grows more than you want. With `BACKUP_DIR` set, a pruned row lives on in the daily snapshots for up to `BACKUP_KEEP` days more.

- **Games played on one device are kept too, with less.** Where the build has a relay (`VITE_MULTIPLAYER_SERVER` – the same condition as the daily board's Leaderboard button; a build without one never sends anything), a local game against CPUs or on a shared keyboard is told to the relay once, when it has been played to its end (`local_game`: the room-style rules, who sat where, and each round as `round_stats` has it). A game that was left halfway is not sent. The relay stores it like any other game's rounds (`stat_rounds`, `stat_seats`, one `stat_matches` row marked `source = 'local'`), with no turns, shots or placements – so there is nothing to fly it again with, and it lists as *Local* with the Rewatch button off. The person is the hash of their address alone (there is no name), its points on the heatmap are the points the seats ended on (no shots to add up), and it counts for the day like any other game. The daily challenge is not sent this way: it has its own board.

### What is stored

`stat_rounds` – one row per round:

| Column | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `room_id` | The room's unique id: random, made with the room, never reused |
| `match_id` | Random id of the game (a room can play several) |
| `round`, `at`, `day` | Round number, Unix time and UTC date it was reported |
| `game_mode`, `mode` | `classic` / `horizon`; `ffa` / `team` |
| `rules` | The room's rules as JSON |
| `humans`, `cpus`, `spectators` | How many seats are people, how many CPUs, how many watch |
| `title`, `survivor`, `team` | How the round ended, the last ship standing, the winning team |
| `seconds`, `planets` | Length of the round, planet count |
| `longest_shot`, `fastest_kill`, `best_hit`, `close_call`, `sniper` (+ `_seat`) | The round's records and the seat that set each |

`stat_seats` – one row per ship and round:

| Column | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `match_id`, `round`, `seat`, `at` | Which ship in which round |
| `player` | The player's hash (address and name); empty for a CPU |
| `cpu` | A CPU's level; empty for a person |
| `team`, `won`, `alive` | Team (team games), won the round, survived it |
| `score`, `shots`, `kills`, `swingbys`, `grazes`, `own_goals`, `pickups`, `saves` | The ship's round |
| `missiles`, `intercepts`, `stray_kills` | The ship's planetary defenses: missiles fired at enemy shots, shots they brought down, and enemy ships hit by a missile that lost its shot and flew on (also among `kills`); empty from a client that does not count them |

`stat_turns` – one row per ship and turn, for how the scores run:

| Column | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `match_id`, `round`, `turn`, `seat`, `at` | Which ship after which turn |
| `player`, `cpu` | The player's hash, or the level of a CPU |
| `alive`, `score` | Whether the ship is still in the round, and its score after the turn |

`stat_shots` – one row per shot, as the host's game logged it:

| Column | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `match_id`, `round`, `turn`, `n`, `at` | Which turn of which round, and the shot's number in the order fired in the round |
| `seat`, `player`, `cpu` | The shooter's seat; the player's hash or the level of a CPU |
| `angle`, `power` | As aimed, to the last decimal: degrees (0° right, counter-clockwise) and power 0–100 |
| `power_up` | What rode with the shot: `preview`, `piercing`, `plasma`, or `splitter` (for its shots); empty for none |
| `fork` | One of the extra shots a splitter made |
| `rider` | What was armed when the shot was fired (`preview`, `piercing`, `plasma`); unlike `power_up` it stays that when the shot is split on the way |
| `fan`, `split_at` | For a splitter: the fan id the shot and its forks share (unique within the turn), and the physics step of the flight at which the splitter went off, the same for the whole fan. Empty for a shot that was never split. A replay splits the aimed shot (`fork` = 0) at that step into the fan's forks |
| `resolution` | How it ended: `ship`, `planet`, `hole` (black hole), `shotdown` (by a defense), `clash` (against another shot), `lost` (off the field), `timeout` |
| `hits`, `hit_seat`, `hit_kind` | Ships it took out (armor piercing can take several), the first of them, and whether it was an `enemy`, `self` or `friendly` |
| `points` | What the shot earned: its hits, a high-five, the multi-kill bonus (negative for a ship of one's own) |

`stat_tips` – one row per tip of the oracle game that was judged, with no name and no seat:

| Column | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `match_id`, `round`, `n`, `at` | Which round, and the tip's number in it |
| `kind` | `shot` (will it hit, or who gets hit) or `round` (who wins the round) |
| `correct` | Whether the tip was right |
| `stake`, `payout` | Points bet on a round tip and what came back, the stake included; 0 and 0 for a plain tip or a lost bet's payout |
| `underdog` | A right tip against the crowd |
| `stands` | Placed from the stands rather than by a shot-down player |

Placement power-ups (teleport, black hole, …) and shield bounces are not shots and are not logged here; a defense's stray missile is counted in the seat's score, not as a shot. All tables can be read with any SQLite tool.

### Facts in the menus

The title menu and the multiplayer lobby show **facts** from the statistics wherever the build has a relay and the player has left the ticker enabled: a continuous news ticker with ten shuffled facts at a time. It highlights numbers, pauses on hover, and is manually scrollable when reduced motion is preferred. The player can turn it off at **Settings → Display → Stats ticker** (German: **Einstellungen → Darstellung → Statistik-Laufband**); when off, the client does not ask the relay for facts. Examples include *"In Event Horizon, 20% of shots at power 80 or more hit the shooter – below that, 4%"* or *"12% of bets pay out more than the stake"*. The client asks the relay with `stats_facts` and is answered with `stats_facts` (`enabled: false` when the relay keeps no statistics): a list of `{ id, values }`, where the numbers are what the sentence is made of and the client words them in the player's language (`fact.<id>` in `src/platform/i18n.ts`, `src/net/statsFacts.ts` for how each number is shown). Each fact is a query of its own (`server/src/stats/facts.rs`) that also counts what it looked at; a fact with too few of them is left out, so a young database tells little instead of boasting of three shots. The facts cover aim and power (shots it takes to destroy a ship, shots lost to the void or swallowed by a black hole, first turn against later ones, repeated aims after a miss, self hits by power and game mode, power-ups), rounds and games (length, self-hit endings, shares of modes, humans against CPUs, swing-bys, records, today's pilots and rounds online and on one device, today's daily challenge) and shot types (plasma, piercing, preview and splitter shots and how often they hit, low-power shots), kills (double kills and better, the first shot's kills, who shoots first in Classic, how often the leader after the first turn loses), the most-placed power-up and planets shot to pieces, planetary defenses (how many shots their missiles bring down, ships hit by stray missiles), who plays (CPU levels, spectators, grazes, pickups, shield saves, self-hits, the week against the week before, weekends) and the oracle (how often tips are right, underdogs, the stands' share, bets that paid, the average and biggest stakes, the biggest win and loss). The relay hands out the facts it has and makes them anew in the background at most every ten minutes (only the first request after a start waits for the queries); the client keeps them for ten, across page loads (`sessionStorage`). Facts that name a single record (the best hit, the fastest kill, the closest call, the longest round and shot, the longest kill, the biggest win and loss) need at least 25 samples, because hosts report their numbers and they are taken at face value. A count needs at least two, and a fact whose numbers are all zero is left out. A shot is one a player fired: a splitter's forks are not shots of their own, but a ship one of them destroys counts as a hit of the shot they came from. The facts are read on a connection of their own, so their scans never queue the overview. Like the overview, only totals leave the relay.

When the ticker is enabled and its ten-minute cache has expired, the menu opens a WebSocket to the relay as soon as it is shown, before someone opens Multiplayer; the relay therefore sees the visitor's IP address earlier than it otherwise would.

### The statistics page

The title menu has a **Statistics** entry wherever the build has a relay (`VITE_MULTIPLAYER_SERVER`), in the menu's own style: a GitHub-style heatmap of the last year, a switch above it between **rounds**, **players** and **points** per day, and under it a table of the latest 50 games and — once somebody has tipped — a **Betting** block (`betting` in `stats_overview`: tips and bets of all kept games added up: share of right round and shot tips, bets that paid more than the stake, average and biggest stake, biggest win, right tips against the crowd, the stands' share, and the points the bets took in or gave out) (when, mode, seats, rounds, points) with a **Rewatch** button at the right of each row. The numbers come over the relay's WebSocket as one more control message, `stats_overview`, answered with `stats_overview` (`enabled: false` when the relay keeps no statistics, so the page says so). Only totals leave the relay – no player hash, no room, no seat – so the message is open to anybody who can connect, like the daily board.

- **Days and games.** The per-day totals live in `stat_days` (rounds; the points the seats earned: each round adds what the scores rose by, betting left out) and `stat_day_players` (the hashes of the people who played that day, counted once per day) and are pruned with everything else after `STATS_KEEP_DAYS`, so the heatmap spans as much as is kept (at most the year it draws). Days are UTC dates. The table lists the latest 50 games.
- **Points** are the points the seats earned: what their scores rose by, round after round, less what betting moved (the shot-down players' tips and bets; the stands' bankroll never counted). A game's points are the seats' final scores less the same.
- **Rewatch flies the game again** – see *Replays* below. The button is on when the relay has the whole game, and its tooltip says why it is off otherwise.

### Replays

A game is not recorded as a film or as a stream of states. The simulation is deterministic (the same world and the same aims fly the same flight, a flight leaves the same pick-ups in the same hands, and the next turn starts from there), so what has to be kept is only what the *players* decided, plus what builds the worlds:

- **`stat_matches`** – one row per game: the `seed` (the relay's own, which the clients now start their match from), the room's `rules`, the `game_mode` and `mode`, and `seats` (a CPU's level and the team, per seat – no names). `replayable` drops to 0 when a client reported a turn without a list of placements, i.e. one that does not log what a replay needs (an older build).
- **`stat_shots`** – per turn, every shot that was aimed (`fork` = 0): its seat, its aim exactly as aimed (`angle`, `power`), the `rider` that was armed, and `split_at`, the physics step of the flight at which the splitter went off. The forks are not stored as inputs – the splitter makes them.
- **`stat_placements`** – per turn, in the order they were made, the power-ups that were laid on the field instead of a shot: the `kind`, where (`x`, `y`, and `x2`, `y2` for a wormhole's second mouth) or on which planet (`planet`, its place in the world's list). A teleport to a spot the game drew itself has none: drawing it again from the same stream gives the same spot, so only the fact is kept. Even a draw that found nowhere to land is kept, because it used the stream.

Nothing else is stored: not the planets, the pick-ups, who picked up what, the scores, the CPUs' decisions or the shield events. The client builds the match from the seed and the rules (`netMatch`, the same function that builds it for a live game), gives it a `script`, and in place of the players' input and the CPUs' planning the match does what the script says at the points where a live match waits for a person or thinks: at the start of an aiming phase it lays the turn's placements and fires its shots, and during the flight it splits the shots that were split, at the step they were split at. Everything else – the flight, the kills, the points, the pick-ups, the next round – is the game itself. Event Horizon's killcams play too and can be skipped. `replayState` says how it went: `finished` at the end of the recording, `drifted` if the match stopped doing what the recording says it did (a version of the game that plays differently from the one that was recorded; there is no version tag on a recording yet).

`tests/replay.test.ts` plays matches with CPUs (every mode, power-ups of every kind, splitters, team games, Mayhem), flies each again from nothing but the script its turn reports make, and requires every turn to come out the same, to the last point; the multiplayer test does the same through the relay and the database.

A game is flyable when its row is there, no client left out the placements, all the rounds of the rules were reported, and every round's turns are all there (a host that left halfway leaves a game that is not). The relay answers `stats_replay` (`id`) with the game, or `replay: null`; the overview marks the flyable ones with `replay: true`. Replays are kept as long as the rest of the statistics (`STATS_KEEP_DAYS`).

### What it costs

Three benchmarks, each with a baseline that has no statistics (`benchmarks/README.md` lists their knobs):

- **`npm run build:server && npm run bench:stats` – the relay.** It starts the relay twice from the same binary, without and with `--stats`, and puts the same load on each: rooms streaming 30 Hz states, hosts reporting turns (with placements) and rounds, a few readers asking for the statistics page's numbers twice a second, a client telling of games played on one device, and at the end one whole game whose replay is fetched. It compares state latency, CPU time, memory, the readers' latency, and rows and bytes written. Reports go to a single writer task through a queue of 1024, one transaction each, so a report never waits for the database and a burst cannot grow memory without bound – if the queue is full the report is dropped with a warning.
- **`npm run bench:client` – the game's own simulation.** Seeded CPU matches in four ways that each add to the one before (the shot log switched off; as it runs; plus the reports an online host builds and packs at the end of every turn and round; plus a local game's one message), against the same match flown again from its recording. It reads the time of one `update` while a shot is in the air, and its tail.
- **`npm run bench:browser` – frame times in headless Chromium** of a build without a relay (nothing is ever sent) against one with it: the title screen, a match, a replay, a plain dialog (to read the statistics page against) and the statistics page itself. Headless Chromium draws in software, so read `on` against `off`, not the milliseconds.

On a development machine (release build, 10 rooms of four, each turn reported every 250 ms with two shots – a real turn takes seconds, so this is about ten times a busy relay's rate, 270 rows/s):

- **The relay** with statistics on used about 0.5 CPU-seconds more per 10 s (0.5 → 1.0) and 4 MB more memory (41 → 45 MB), and state latency barely moved (p99 1.8 → 1.9 ms). The statistics page's readers were answered in 0.6 ms (p50) and 7 ms (p99) while it was being written to, and one replay (eight turns, 2 KB) was fetched in about 1 ms. The file grew by about 0.4 MB/s at that rate, 1.6 KB per row.
- **The game**: one `update` while a shot is in the air takes about 10 µs (p99 about 50 µs) with or without the shot log, and nothing was near the 16.7 ms of a frame; building and packing a turn's report is about 0.1 ms and a match's whole set of reports under 5 KB. Flying a match again from its recording takes 4–7 % of the time of playing it, because nothing has to think.

The benchmark found a real problem on the way: with a few readers on the statistics page the relay answered in seconds (p50 4 s) while it was being written to, because the readers' queries waited on one another on one connection. The relay now makes the overview at most once every two seconds, by one reader at a time, and hands the same one to everybody who asks in that time.
